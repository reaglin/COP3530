Introduction

Lists and arrays are the starting point for almost everything else in this course. An array is an ordered sequence of elements stored under a single name, where each element is reached by a numeric index that normally begins at 0. That simple idea — position-based access — is what makes arrays fast for some tasks (reading or writing a known position) and slower for others (inserting or deleting in the middle, which may require shifting elements). A linked list stores the same kind of ordered data differently, chaining nodes together with references, which trades fast indexing for cheaper insertion and deletion.

In JavaScript, arrays are flexible: they can grow and shrink, hold mixed types, and come with built-in methods you will use constantly in this module. Before you can analyze or optimize array algorithms, you need comfort with the mechanics — length, bracket notation, zero-based indexing, and how out-of-range access behaves. That fluency is the foundation for the insertion, deletion, search, and traversal work that follows in Module 3.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, what exactly does indexing cost compared with searching? Second, when would you prefer a linked structure over a contiguous array? Third, how does the ordered, indexed nature of an array shape the algorithms we can write on top of it? Jot down the trade-offs you hear; they reappear in the Big O discussions later in the module.
