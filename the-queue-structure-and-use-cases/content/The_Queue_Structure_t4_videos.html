Introduction

A queue is an abstract data type defined by its behavior: elements enter at the rear and leave from the front, in first-in-first-out order. Knowing that behavior is not the same as being able to build it. In this topic you move from the concept to the code, implementing the queue two different ways — on top of a fixed-size array and on top of a linked list — and seeing exactly what each choice costs you.

The array-based version is usually the first one students write, and it is also the one that exposes the most interesting problems. Where do front and rear pointers start? What happens when rear reaches the end of the array but space has been freed at the front? How do you distinguish an empty queue from a full one? The linked list version avoids the fixed-capacity problem but introduces pointer bookkeeping of its own, especially when the queue becomes empty and both head and tail must be reset.

As you watch, keep a running list of three things: the exact conditions that signal overflow and underflow, the pointer updates performed by enqueue and dequeue, and the time and space cost of each operation. You should finish this topic able to argue for one implementation over the other given a specific scenario, not just recite that both are O(1) for the core operations.
