Introduction

Queues and stacks are both linear structures that restrict where you may insert and remove elements, but they restrict them in opposite ways. A queue follows FIFO (first in, first out): items enter at the rear and leave from the front, so the element that has waited longest is served first. A stack follows LIFO (last in, first out): items are pushed and popped at a single end, so the most recently added element is removed first. That single difference in the point of access is what drives everything else about how the two structures behave.

This comparison matters because in practice you rarely choose a structure by its name; you choose it by the ordering behavior a problem demands. Scheduling, buffering, breadth-first traversal, and any "serve in arrival order" situation point toward a queue. Undo histories, expression evaluation, backtracking, recursion and call stacks, and depth-first traversal point toward a stack. Being able to justify that choice is as important in this course as being able to code either structure.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the vocabulary of the operations (push/pop versus enqueue/dequeue), where the pointers or indices live (top versus front and rear), and the real-world examples used to motivate each structure. The third video goes a step further and shows that the two structures are related closely enough that one can be simulated with the other — a useful way to confirm that you really understand what each ordering principle costs you.
