Introduction to the Queue Data Structure

Introduction

A queue is one of the most intuitive data structures you will meet in this course, because you already use one every time you wait in line. Formally, a queue is an ordered collection in which elements are added at one end, called the rear or tail, and removed from the other end, called the front or head. This single restriction produces the First-In, First-Out (FIFO) ordering principle: the element that has waited the longest is the next one to leave.

In COP3530 the queue matters for two reasons. First, it is a clean example of an abstract data type (ADT): the interface — enqueue, dequeue, peek, isEmpty — is defined by behavior, not by any particular implementation, and the same interface can be built on an array or on a linked list. Second, queues appear constantly inside larger algorithms and systems, from CPU and print scheduling to breadth-first search on graphs, so understanding them now pays off later in the course.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. What exactly happens to the front and rear pointers during an enqueue and a dequeue? Which conditions count as edge cases (empty queue, full queue, a single remaining element)? And how does the queue's access pattern differ from a stack's, where insertion and removal happen at the same end? Being able to answer those questions in your own words is the goal of this topic.

The FIFO Principle

Introduction

A queue is one of the simplest data structures to describe and one of the easiest to misuse if you do not take its ordering rule seriously. That rule is FIFO: First-In, First-Out. The first element placed into the queue is the first element taken out, and no operation is allowed to violate that order. Insertion happens at one end (the rear, via enqueue) and removal happens at the other (the front, via dequeue), which is what keeps the ordering honest.

FIFO matters in this course for two reasons. First, it is a defining constraint, not a convenience: the queue is interesting precisely because it refuses to let you reach into the middle. That restriction is what makes it predictable and what makes it the right tool for scheduling, buffering, print spooling, breadth-first traversal, and any situation where arrival order should determine service order. Second, FIFO becomes much clearer when you set it beside LIFO, the stack's ordering rule, where insertion and removal share a single end.

While watching, pay attention to three things: where elements enter and where they leave, how the front and rear positions move as operations occur, and how the stack's single point of access produces reversed output compared to the queue's preserved order. If you can predict the exact sequence of outputs for a given sequence of operations on both structures, you understand the principle.

Queue Operations

Introduction

A queue is an abstract data type that organizes elements in first-in, first-out (FIFO) order: items join at one end, called the rear or tail, and leave from the other end, called the front or head. What makes the queue useful is not a clever internal layout but the small, strictly defined set of operations it exposes. In this topic you study those operations one at a time — enqueue, dequeue, peek (front), and isEmpty — and learn exactly what each one does to the structure's state.

These operations matter because every later queue topic depends on them. When you implement a queue with an array or a linked list, you are really deciding how to make these four behaviors correct and efficient. Edge cases live here too: dequeueing or peeking an empty queue (underflow) and enqueueing into a full fixed-size array (overflow) are the conditions that separate working code from code that silently corrupts data.

As you watch, track two things. First, the front and rear indices or pointers: note precisely when each one moves, and what values they hold when the queue is empty versus holding one element. Second, the expected outcome of each operation — what is returned, what changes, and what error or condition is signaled when the operation cannot be performed.

Implementing a Queue

Introduction

A queue is an abstract data type defined by its behavior: elements enter at the rear and leave from the front, in first-in-first-out order. Knowing that behavior is not the same as being able to build it. In this topic you move from the concept to the code, implementing the queue two different ways — on top of a fixed-size array and on top of a linked list — and seeing exactly what each choice costs you.

The array-based version is usually the first one students write, and it is also the one that exposes the most interesting problems. Where do front and rear pointers start? What happens when rear reaches the end of the array but space has been freed at the front? How do you distinguish an empty queue from a full one? The linked list version avoids the fixed-capacity problem but introduces pointer bookkeeping of its own, especially when the queue becomes empty and both head and tail must be reset.

As you watch, keep a running list of three things: the exact conditions that signal overflow and underflow, the pointer updates performed by enqueue and dequeue, and the time and space cost of each operation. You should finish this topic able to argue for one implementation over the other given a specific scenario, not just recite that both are O(1) for the core operations.

Real-World Applications of Queues

Introduction

A queue is more than a classroom exercise in enqueue and dequeue. Nearly every system you interact with daily relies on something arriving first being served first: jobs waiting for a CPU, documents waiting for a printer, packets waiting for a network card, customers waiting for a support agent. In this topic we take the FIFO principle you learned abstractly and trace it through real systems so you can recognize a queue when you meet one in the wild.

The subtopics each highlight a different reason FIFO is the right fit. Task scheduling uses it for fairness and predictable wait times. Print spooling and data buffering use it to decouple a fast producer from a slow consumer. Message queues in distributed systems use it so that services can hand off work reliably without being online at the same moment. Breadth-first search uses it to explore a graph level by level, which is a purely algorithmic use with no waiting line in sight. Customer service and ticketing systems use it because people expect order of arrival to be respected.

As you watch the videos below, keep two questions in mind. First, what would break if the ordering guarantee were removed — would the result merely be unfair, or would it be wrong? Second, where is the queue actually stored, and who is producing and who is consuming? Being able to answer those two questions is what separates memorizing FIFO from being able to choose a queue for a problem you have never seen before.

Queues vs. Stacks: A Comparison

Introduction

Queues and stacks are both linear structures that restrict where you may insert and remove elements, but they restrict them in opposite ways. A queue follows FIFO (first in, first out): items enter at the rear and leave from the front, so the element that has waited longest is served first. A stack follows LIFO (last in, first out): items are pushed and popped at a single end, so the most recently added element is removed first. That single difference in the point of access is what drives everything else about how the two structures behave.

This comparison matters because in practice you rarely choose a structure by its name; you choose it by the ordering behavior a problem demands. Scheduling, buffering, breadth-first traversal, and any "serve in arrival order" situation point toward a queue. Undo histories, expression evaluation, backtracking, recursion and call stacks, and depth-first traversal point toward a stack. Being able to justify that choice is as important in this course as being able to code either structure.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the vocabulary of the operations (push/pop versus enqueue/dequeue), where the pointers or indices live (top versus front and rear), and the real-world examples used to motivate each structure. The third video goes a step further and shows that the two structures are related closely enough that one can be simulated with the other — a useful way to confirm that you really understand what each ordering principle costs you.
