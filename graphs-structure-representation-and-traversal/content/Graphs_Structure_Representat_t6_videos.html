Introduction

Depth-First Search (DFS) is one of the two fundamental ways to systematically visit every vertex in a graph. The strategy is simple to state: from the current vertex, follow one edge as deep as you can, and only when you run out of unvisited neighbors do you back up (backtrack) and try another branch. That "go deep first" behavior is what separates DFS from BFS, which fans out level by level.

In this course, DFS matters for two reasons. First, it is a building block: cycle detection, topological sorting, finding connected components, path existence checks, and maze/puzzle solving are all DFS in disguise. Second, it is a good test of your understanding of recursion and explicit stacks — the recursive version hides the stack in the call stack, while the iterative version makes that stack visible, and the two can produce different (but equally valid) visit orders.

As you watch, keep a short checklist in mind: Where is the visited set maintained, and when is a vertex marked visited (on push, or on pop)? How is backtracking actually happening? What happens if the graph is disconnected — how does the algorithm restart from an unvisited vertex? And how would the same pseudocode map onto an adjacency list in your own Graph class? Try tracing each example by hand on paper before the video reveals the answer.
