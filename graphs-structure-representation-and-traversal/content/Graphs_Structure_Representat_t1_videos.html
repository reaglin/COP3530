Introduction

A graph is a data structure made of vertices (also called nodes) connected by edges. Unlike arrays, lists, or trees, graphs place no restriction on how items may connect: any vertex can link to any number of others, connections can be one-way or two-way, and cycles are allowed. That flexibility is exactly why graphs model so many real systems — road networks, social connections, web links, course prerequisites, and dependency chains in software.

In this course, graphs are the structure that ties together much of what you have already learned. Before you can implement adjacency lists or trace BFS and DFS, you need a precise vocabulary: vertex, edge, directed versus undirected, weighted versus unweighted, adjacency, degree, path, cycle, and connected versus disconnected. Getting these terms right early makes the later material about representation and traversal far easier to follow, because every algorithm description assumes them.

As you watch, focus on three things. First, how each video draws a graph and names its parts, so you can read a diagram quickly. Second, the difference an arrow makes — what changes when an edge is directed. Third, the terminology around moving through a graph (walks, paths, cycles), since traversal algorithms are formally described in those terms. Try sketching a small graph of your own, such as five friends and who follows whom, and label it using the vocabulary from each video.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes
