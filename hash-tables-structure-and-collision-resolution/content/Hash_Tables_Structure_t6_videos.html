Introduction

Hash tables are often described as offering "constant time" lookup, insertion, and deletion, but that claim comes with conditions. The O(1) average case depends on a hash function that spreads keys evenly, a load factor that is kept under control, and a collision resolution strategy that does not degenerate. When any of those assumptions breaks down, performance slides toward O(n) — the same cost as scanning a plain array.

In this topic you will work through what actually drives hash table performance: the load factor (the ratio of stored entries to table slots), how often collisions occur, and how chaining and open addressing each respond as the table fills. You will also look at the space side of the trade-off — the extra pointers chaining requires, the empty slots open addressing needs to stay fast, and the amortized cost of resizing and rehashing when the table grows.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, what operation is being performed at each step, and how many probes or list traversals does it take? Second, how would that count change if the table were nearly full versus mostly empty? Third, what input would force the worst case — and why does that matter for real systems that accept untrusted keys?

Video 3: L-6.4: Linear Probing in Hashing with example

Gate Smashers · 12:40 · 945,731 views · 5y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

A longer, step-by-step treatment of linear probing as an open addressing scheme, where collisions are resolved by scanning forward to the next free slot. The extended running time allows for detailed insertion and search examples, and the video is likely to illustrate clustering — the way occupied slots group together and lengthen subsequent probe sequences. Deletion and its complications under probing are commonly addressed in lectures of this kind.

Why this video

Open addressing degrades much more sharply as the load factor approaches 1 than chaining does, and seeing probe sequences worked out slot by slot makes that divergence concrete rather than abstract.
