Introduction

Big O notation is the standard language for describing how an algorithm's running time or memory use grows as the input size grows. Rather than measuring seconds on a particular machine, Big O captures an upper bound on the rate of growth, which lets us compare algorithms in a way that stays meaningful across hardware, languages, and compilers. In this topic we revisit that idea carefully: what the notation actually asserts, the common growth classes you will see again and again (constant, logarithmic, linear, linearithmic, quadratic, exponential), and why constants and lower-order terms are dropped when only the dominant term matters at scale.

This matters throughout COP3530 because nearly every data structure decision you make is justified with a complexity argument. When you choose a hash table over a sorted array, or a balanced tree over a linked list, you are claiming something about growth rates. Reading Big O fluently also means recognizing patterns in code: a single loop over n elements, nested loops, a loop that halves the search space each iteration, or a recursive call structure each map to a predictable class.

As you watch, pay attention to three things. First, how each presenter defines Big O formally versus how they use it informally. Second, how they move from a piece of code to a growth expression and then simplify it. Third, the connection between Big O and worst-case analysis — Big O is often used for worst-case guarantees, but the notation itself and the case being analyzed are two separate choices, and keeping them distinct will save you confusion later in this module.
