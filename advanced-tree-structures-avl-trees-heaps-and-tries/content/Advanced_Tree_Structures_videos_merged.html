Review of Binary Search Trees and Their Limitations

Introduction

A binary search tree (BST) organizes data so that, at every node, all keys in the left subtree are smaller and all keys in the right subtree are larger. That single ordering invariant is what makes search, insertion, and deletion feel fast: at each step you discard half of the remaining possibilities, much like binary search on a sorted array. Before moving on to AVL trees, heaps, and tries, we need to be precise about what a BST guarantees and, just as importantly, what it does not.

The catch is that BST performance depends on shape, not just on size. Operations cost O(h), where h is the height of the tree. A well-proportioned tree over n keys has height around log n, but a tree built by inserting already-sorted (or reverse-sorted) data degenerates into a path essentially a linked list and height becomes n. This is the gap between the average case and the worst case that motivates every structure in this module: if we can force the tree to stay shallow, we get logarithmic behavior regardless of input order.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, how is each operation (search, insert, delete) expressed recursively in terms of the ordering invariant? Second, where exactly does the cost of an operation come from, and how does it relate to height? Third, which insertion sequences produce balanced versus degenerate trees? Try sketching a few insertion orders on paper and comparing the resulting shapes before you reach the AVL material.

AVL Trees and Self-Balancing Mechanisms

Introduction

A binary search tree gives you O(log n) search, insert, and delete only when it stays reasonably short. Insert keys in sorted or nearly sorted order and the same tree degenerates into what is essentially a linked list, pushing every operation to O(n). AVL trees fix this by adding one simple rule: for every node, the heights of its left and right subtrees may differ by at most one. That difference is the node's balance factor, and whenever an insertion or deletion violates the rule, the tree repairs itself with local restructuring operations called rotations.

In this topic you will learn the AVL definition and its height guarantee, how to compute and update balance factors, and the four repair cases: single left and single right rotations for the "straight line" imbalances, and the left-right and right-left double rotations for the "zig-zag" cases. Insertion requires at most one rotation (single or double) to restore balance; deletion may require rebalancing at several nodes along the path back to the root, which is why it is treated separately.

The three videos below form a short, ordered sequence: definition and search, then insertion, then deletion. While watching, keep a pen handy and redraw each example yourself. Focus on three things: which node is the first unbalanced ancestor, which of the four cases applies based on the direction of the imbalance, and how heights and balance factors are updated after the rotation. Pausing to predict the rotation before the narrator performs it is the fastest way to build fluency for exams.

AVL Tree Performance and Use Cases

Introduction

An AVL tree is a binary search tree that enforces a balance invariant: for every node, the heights of its left and right subtrees differ by at most one. That single structural guarantee is what turns the shaky worst case of an ordinary BST into a dependable one. Because the height of an AVL tree with n keys stays proportional to log n, search, insert, and remove are all O(log n) in the worst case, not just on average.

This topic asks you to reason about that guarantee rather than just state it. You will look at where the log n bound comes from, what the extra balance information costs in memory (a height or balance factor stored per node, plus the rotations performed during updates), and how AVL trees compare to plain BSTs, to other balanced trees such as red-black trees, and to hash tables. Each comparison has a different flavor: plain BSTs are cheaper to update but can degrade to a linked list on sorted input; red-black trees rebalance less aggressively and so insert slightly faster but search slightly slower; hash tables beat both on average lookup time but give up ordering and worst-case predictability.

As you watch, keep two questions in front of you. First, is the claim being made about the average case or the worst case? That distinction is the whole argument for AVL trees in lookup-intensive and latency-sensitive systems, where a predictable upper bound matters more than a good average. Second, what does the structure give up to earn its guarantee? Write down the trade-off in your own words for each structure you see, because the final learning outcome in this module is about justifying a choice, not memorizing a table.

Heap Trees and Priority Queues

Introduction

A heap is a complete binary tree that satisfies an ordering rule between every parent and its children: in a max-heap each parent is greater than or equal to its children, and in a min-heap each parent is less than or equal to its children. Notice what the heap property does NOT guarantee — there is no left-to-right ordering as in a binary search tree, so searching for an arbitrary value is still O(n). What you do get, cheaply and reliably, is constant-time access to the single most extreme element at the root, plus O(log n) insertion and removal. Because the tree is always complete, a heap needs no pointers at all: it can be packed into an array where the children of index i live at 2i+1 and 2i+2, and the parent at (i-1)/2.

That combination is exactly what a priority queue needs. A priority queue serves elements by priority rather than arrival order, and it underpins job schedulers, event simulation, Huffman coding, and the frontier in Dijkstra's and A* search algorithms — structures you will meet again later in this course and in algorithms courses. The two core maintenance routines are bubble-up (sift-up), used after appending a new element at the end of the array, and bubble-down (sift-down), used after moving the last element to the root when the root is extracted. Building a heap from an unordered array with heapify applies sift-down from the last internal node backwards, which runs in O(n) rather than the O(n log n) you might expect from n separate insertions.

As you watch, keep a pencil moving. Draw the tree and the array side by side, and after every swap check that both views still agree. Pay attention to when the speaker compares against a parent versus against children, how far a sifted element can travel (at most the height of the tree, hence O(log n)), and how the same two routines get reused for insertion, removal, heapify, and heapsort. Also note which videos use 0-based versus 1-based indexing, since the parent and child formulas change slightly between the two conventions.

Heap Operations and Performance Trade-offs

Introduction

A binary heap is a complete binary tree that satisfies the heap property: in a min-heap every parent is no larger than its children, and in a max-heap every parent is no smaller. Because the tree is complete, we can store it in a plain array and compute relationships arithmetically — for a node at index i (0-based), its children sit at 2i+1 and 2i+2 and its parent at floor((i-1)/2). This array trick is what makes heaps fast and memory-efficient, and it is the reason heaps are the standard implementation behind priority queues.

The operations you need to master are few but must be traced carefully. Insert places a new element at the end of the array and sifts it up while it violates the heap property; extract-min or extract-max removes the root, moves the last element into the root slot, and sifts down; heapify restores the property at a subtree, and repeated heapify from the middle of the array builds a heap from unordered data. Insert and extract run in O(log n), peeking at the root is O(1), and building a heap bottom-up is O(n).

As you watch, keep two questions in mind. First, at every swap, what are the index computations — which child is chosen for comparison, and why? Second, how does a heap compare to alternatives such as a sorted array, an unsorted array, or a balanced BST: a heap gives you cheap access to the extreme element but does not support fast arbitrary search or ordered traversal, which is exactly the trade-off that decides when to use one.

Trie Structures for String Storage and Retrieval

Introduction

A trie (also called a prefix tree) is a tree structure built specifically for storing strings. Instead of storing an entire key at a single node, a trie spreads a string across a path: each edge or node position corresponds to one character, and a word is represented by the sequence of characters encountered from the root down to a node marked as the end of a word. Because strings that begin the same way share the same initial path, tries naturally group words by common prefix.

This matters in COP3530 because tries trade memory for predictable speed. Lookup in a balanced BST costs O(log n) comparisons of whole strings, while a trie lookup costs roughly O(m) steps for a string of length m, independent of how many words are stored. That makes tries the structure behind autocomplete, spell-checking, IP routing tables, and dictionary word games. The cost is space: every node may hold an array or map of child pointers, one slot per possible character.

As you watch, pay close attention to four things: how a node is represented (child array versus hash map, plus the end-of-word flag), how insertion walks down the tree creating nodes only when a child is missing, how search distinguishes "this prefix exists" from "this complete word exists," and how deletion must avoid removing nodes that are still needed by other words. Try tracing a small word set such as cat, car, card, and dog by hand on paper, then check your drawing against the walkthroughs in the videos.

Comparing Advanced Tree Structures: Use Cases and Trade-offs

Introduction

So far in this module you have studied AVL trees, heaps, and tries one at a time, learning how each is built and how its operations work. This topic shifts the question from "how does it work?" to "when should I use it?" Each of these structures buys a particular guarantee at a particular cost: AVL trees pay for rotations and extra height bookkeeping in exchange for guaranteed O(log n) search on ordered data; heaps give up full ordering and fast arbitrary search in exchange for O(1) access to the minimum or maximum; tries trade significant memory for lookups whose cost depends on key length rather than the number of stored keys.

This matters because real engineering decisions are rarely about which structure is "best" in the abstract. They are about matching a structure's strengths to the operations your problem performs most often. A scheduler that repeatedly asks for the next highest-priority task, an index that must support ordered range queries, and an autocomplete box that resolves prefixes are three different problems with three different right answers. Being able to justify that choice — with complexity bounds and practical considerations like memory overhead and cache behavior — is the skill this topic develops.

As you watch the videos below, keep a comparison table in front of you with rows for AVL trees, heaps, and tries and columns for search, insert, delete, min/max access, space, and typical use cases. Note where the videos confirm what you already know, and pay attention to where real systems actually deploy these structures. Also watch for the common pitfalls: assuming a heap can search efficiently, assuming a balanced BST is always worth its rotation cost, and underestimating the memory footprint of a trie over a large alphabet.
