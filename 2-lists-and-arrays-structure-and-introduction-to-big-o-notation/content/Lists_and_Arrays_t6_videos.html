Introduction

Big O notation is the standard vocabulary computer scientists use to describe how the cost of an algorithm grows as the size of its input grows. Instead of measuring runtime in seconds — which depends on your machine, your compiler, and what else is running — Big O describes the shape of the growth curve: does the work stay flat as data grows (O(1)), grow in step with the data (O(n)), or explode as the data grows (O(n^2))?

In this module, Big O is the tool that lets you compare arrays and lists honestly. Accessing an element by index in an array is a constant-time operation; searching an unsorted list means potentially touching every element; nested loops over the same collection produce quadratic behavior. Once you can label these patterns, you can justify choosing one structure over another instead of guessing.

As you watch, focus on three habits. First, learn to count operations in terms of n rather than in absolute numbers. Second, practice dropping constants and non-dominant terms — why 3n + 50 is simply O(n), and why n^2 + n collapses to O(n^2). Third, build intuition for the relative ordering of complexity classes so that, when you later analyze insertion or deletion at the front versus the back of a structure, the Big O label comes naturally.
